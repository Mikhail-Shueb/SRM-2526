\begin{abstract}
This report details the implementation, tuning, and verification of the Remote Center of Motion (RCM) controller for a 7-DOF KUKA LBR MED robot carrying a $15\text{ cm}$ biopsy needle tool. Designed specifically for minimally invasive surgery, the control system enforces a strict spatial constraint at the patient's entry incision (the trocar), while simultaneously tracking a multi-point 3D target path inside the body. By tuning the Closed-Loop Inverse Kinematics (CLIK) controller gains and damping factors, we achieved sub-millimeter tracking accuracy ($<0.19\text{ mm}$ peak trajectory error). The mathematical derivations, controller architecture, and simulation results are explained in detailed, beginner-friendly terms.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction to Remote Center of Motion (RCM)}
In minimally invasive surgery (MIS), the robot inserts a long, rigid surgical tool (like a needle or laparoscope) into the patient's body through a tiny skin incision called a \textbf{trocar}. 

To prevent tearing the patient's skin and causing tissue damage, the tool shaft must remain perfectly centered in this incision point. This physical constraint is called a **Remote Center of Motion (RCM)**. It is ``remote'' because the center of rotation is not located at any physical joint of the robot arm; instead, it is a virtual point in space suspended along the needle shaft.

In control terms, this means the robot is restricted: it cannot translate the needle shaft sideways at the trocar. It can only slide the needle forward/backward (insertion depth) and pitch/yaw the needle around the trocar center. This report documents how we control the 7-DOF KUKA LBR MED robot to accomplish this task.

\section{Workspace Setup and Target Trajectory}

\subsection{Needle and Trocar Parameters}
The physical parameters of our simulation are defined as:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Needle Length ($L$):} $0.15\text{ m}$ ($15\text{ cm}$).
    \item \textbf{Trocar Position ($c_r$):} A fixed point in the robot's workspace coordinates:
    \begin{equation}
        c_r = \begin{bmatrix} -0.5 \\ 0.0 \\ 0.4 \end{bmatrix} \text{ meters}
    \end{equation}
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Optimized 3D Target Path}
The trajectory is planned across 6 spatial targets ($m_0$ to $m_5$) inside the patient. These points were carefully selected to cover a rich 3D workspace (varying in depth and lateral width) while remaining within the reachable limits of the $15\text{ cm}$ needle:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $m_0 = [-0.50; \phantom{-}0.00; 0.30]\text{ m}$ (Starting point)
    \item $m_1 = [-0.50; \phantom{-}0.08; 0.28]\text{ m}$
    \item $m_2 = [-0.52; \phantom{-}0.04; 0.27]\text{ m}$ (Deepest insertion target)
    \item $m_3 = [-0.46; -0.04; 0.32]\text{ m}$ (Highest/shallowest target)
    \item $m_4 = [-0.50; -0.08; 0.28]\text{ m}$
    \item $m_5 = [-0.55; \phantom{-}0.00; 0.30]\text{ m}$ (Final target and hold)
\end{itemize}

The simulation runs for 30 seconds total, transitioning smoothly from target to target every 5 seconds, followed by a static 5-second hold at $m_5$.

\section{Kinematics and Constraints (Simple Explanation)}

\subsection{Calculating the Needle Tip Position}
The robot's hand (flange) is located at position $p_e$, and its orientation is represented by a rotation matrix $R_e$. The third column of $R_e$ represents the local Z-axis direction of the hand, which we define as the direction vector $\boldsymbol{z}_e$.

Since the needle is straight and attached to the hand, its tip position ($p_{tool}$) is simply:
\begin{equation}
    p_{tool} = p_e + L \boldsymbol{z}_e
\end{equation}
This means: ``start at the hand position, and walk $15\text{ cm}$ in the direction the hand is pointing.''

\subsection{Formulating the Trocar Constraint Error}
At any instant, the trocar center $c_r$ must lie on the needle shaft. We calculate the distance the needle is inserted through the trocar (the insertion depth $\lambda$) by projecting the trocar vector onto the needle direction vector:
\begin{equation}
    \lambda = (c_r - p_e)^T \boldsymbol{z}_e
\end{equation}
The closest point on the needle shaft to the trocar is:
\begin{equation}
    p_c = p_e + \lambda \boldsymbol{z}_e
\end{equation}
The distance between the trocar center and our needle shaft is the RCM constraint error:
\begin{equation}
    e_{rcm} = c_r - p_c
\end{equation}
Our primary control goal is to keep this error magnitude $\|e_{rcm}\|$ at zero.

\section{Closed-Loop Inverse Kinematics (CLIK) Controller}

\subsection{Task-Priority Framework}
Since the robot has 7 joints (redundancy) but our task space has fewer dimensions, we can prioritize our objectives:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Primary Task (Safety):} Keep the trocar constraint error at zero.
    \item \textbf{Secondary Task (Tracking):} Move the needle tip to follow the trajectory.
\end{enumerate}

To do this, we calculate the joint velocities $\dot{q}$ in two parts:
\begin{equation}
    \dot{q} = \dot{q}_{rcm} + \dot{q}_{tooltip}
\end{equation}

The primary velocity $\dot{q}_{rcm}$ drives the trocar error to zero:
\begin{equation}
    \dot{q}_{rcm} = J_{rcm}^{\dagger} (K_{rcm} e_{rcm})
\end{equation}
where $J_{rcm}^{\dagger}$ is the pseudoinverse of the constraint Jacobian and $K_{rcm}$ is the safety gain.

The secondary velocity $\dot{q}_{tooltip}$ tracks the target path. Crucially, we filter this velocity through a **Null-Space Projection Matrix** $(I - J_{rcm}^{\dagger} J_{rcm})$:
\begin{equation}
    \dot{q}_{tooltip} = (I - J_{rcm}^{\dagger} J_{rcm}) J_{tool\_projected}^{\dagger} \boldsymbol{v}_{required}
\end{equation}
The Null-Space Projection acts as a ``mathematical shield'': it filters out any joint motion that would disturb the trocar. This guarantees that the secondary tracking job never violates the primary safety job.

\subsection{Controller Parameter Tuning}
During initial testing with standard parameters ($K_{tool} = 5$, $\lambda_{damp} = 0.1$), the robot suffered from significant tracking lag (up to $13.5\text{ mm}$ error) because the controller was overly cautious. We resolved this by tuning two parameters:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Increased Tooltip Gain ($K_{tool} = 20$):} This acts like a tighter spring pulling the needle tip toward the target, forcing the controller to correct errors much faster.
    \item \textbf{Decreased Damping ($\lambda_{damp} = 0.01$):} Damping acts like a brake pedal to keep joint velocities stable near singular configurations. By lowering it from $0.1$ to $0.01$, we released the ``brakes,'' allowing the robot to move freely and follow the fast dynamic path.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Note on initial \texorpdfstring{$t = 0$}{t = 0} micro-error}
A microscopic error is visible at the very start ($0.011\text{ mm}$ for tooltip and $0.007\text{ mm}$ for trocar). This is an artifact of the Simulink block parameters: the initial joint positions are entered as a rounded text string (`[0; 0.4085; 3.1416; ...]`) which introduces a tiny truncation discrepancy. This is physically negligible (equivalent to $1/10$th of a human hair width) and is corrected immediately as the simulation starts.

\section{Comparative Analysis: CLIK vs. Analytical Inverse Kinematics}
To evaluate the strengths and weaknesses of the Closed-Loop Inverse Kinematics (CLIK) controller against the analytical Inverse Kinematics (IK) solver, we compare their performance across three critical criteria: path planning, path tracing (RCM enforcement), and error characteristics.

\subsection{Path Planning and Computational Complexity}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Analytical IK:} Provides a direct, closed-form mapping from Cartesian coordinates to joint angles ($q$) at a specific time step. While mathematically instantaneous for a single target point, continuous path planning requires evaluating these complex trigonometric equations at thousands of intermediate points. If the elbow parameter ($\psi$) is not chosen carefully, joints can experience large discontinuities (flips) between consecutive steps.
    \item \textbf{CLIK:} Resolves kinematics at the velocity level ($\dot{q} = J^{\dagger} v$) by integrating joint velocities over time. This naturally produces smooth, continuous joint trajectories. The path planning is embedded directly within the control loop, eliminating the need for pre-calculating the entire joint-space path.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Path Tracing and RCM Constraint Enforcement}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Analytical IK (Open-Loop):} Enforces the RCM constraint by geometrically defining the desired flange position and orientation relative to the trocar. Because it lacks feedback, it cannot correct for unexpected joint drifts, link elasticity, or external forces. If a target is slightly out of reach, the equations yield complex numbers, causing the solver to fail. The trajectory tracked under analytical IK in ideal conditions is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:ik_trajectory_3d}.
    \item \textbf{CLIK (Closed-Loop Feedback):} Treats the RCM constraint as a prioritized task. The controller continuously measures the physical distance from the trocar to the needle shaft and corrects any deviations. This feedback mechanism makes the system extremely robust to initial alignment errors, sensor noise, or physical disturbances.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Error Degree and Frequency}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Error Degree:} Under ideal, noise-free simulation conditions, analytical IK yields zero theoretical tracking and RCM error (assuming perfect joint execution), as shown by the RCM error over time in Fig.~\ref{fig:ik_rcm_error}. In contrast, CLIK introduces tiny, sub-millimeter transient tracking errors ($<0.19\text{ mm}$ for tooltip and $<0.11\text{ mm}$ for trocar) during rapid movements due to controller gain limits and pseudoinverse damping.
    \item \textbf{Error Frequency:} Analytical IK is susceptible to catastrophic, high-frequency errors. If the path passes close to a singularity (e.g. fully stretched elbow $q_4 = 0$), joint velocities spike toward infinity. CLIK resolves this smoothly: the damping factor ($\lambda_{damp} = 0.01$) acts as a soft brake, allowing the robot to pass near singularities by slightly degrading tracking accuracy transiently rather than failing.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
Phase II of this project has successfully demonstrated, analyzed, and compared the design of a Remote Center of Motion (RCM) controller for minimally invasive surgical procedures using the KUKA LBR iiwa 7 robot. The key conclusions and findings from this work are summarized as follows:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Workspace and Tracking Optimization:} By optimizing the workspace target points ($m_2$, $m_3$) and tuning the controller gains ($K_{tool} = 20, \lambda_{damp} = 0.01$), the Closed-Loop Inverse Kinematics (CLIK) controller successfully achieved sub-millimeter tracking accuracy. The maximum transient tooltip error was kept below $0.19\text{ mm}$, and the maximum trocar constraint displacement was restricted to $0.11\text{ mm}$, yielding exact zero-error tracking in steady-state hold.
    \item \textbf{Joint Trajectory Smoothness:} A comparative analysis of joint profiles revealed that analytical IK is highly prone to catastrophic joint-flip discontinuities (sudden $2\pi$ shifts or configuration branch jumps) when solving independent target coordinates. In contrast, the velocity-level integration of the CLIK controller inherently guarantees smooth, continuous joint trajectories, which is a critical requirement for physical robot hardware and patient safety.
    \item \textbf{Closed-Loop vs. Open-Loop Trocar Safety:} While analytical IK can achieve mathematically zero error in ideal noise-free simulations, it is an open-loop method lacking active correction. The task-priority framework of CLIK actively regulates the trocar constraint, ensuring that external disturbances, sensor noise, or physical offsets are dynamically rejected.
    \item \textbf{Robustness to Kinematic Singularities:} Near singular configurations (such as the fully stretched elbow configuration, $q_4 = 0$), analytical IK equations fail due to division by zero, causing command spikes. The damped least-squares pseudo-inverse in the CLIK controller acts as a soft brake, allowing the manipulator to traverse close to singularities with bounded velocities, at the cost of a minor, transient tracking error.
\end{enumerate}
